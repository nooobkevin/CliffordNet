Revisiting Wedge Products in Subspace Clifford Networks

Matched controls, SiLU dead-zones, and mixed-precision stability when scaling Clifford vision backbones.

Code & logs: nooobkevin/CliffordNet Discussion: ParaMind2025/CAN#8 Papers: 2601.06793 & 2610.37226

Subspace Clifford Networks (arXiv:2610.37226) replace standard MLP blocks in vision backbones with a local geometric product computed inside small k-dimensional channel groups (k ∈ {2, 3, 4}). Each block computes two interactions between a state vector u and a depthwise-filtered context vector v: a diagonal inner branch SiLU(u ⊙ v) and an antisymmetric wedge branch u ∧ v.

That design raises one concrete question: under a fixed parameter budget, what does adding the wedge product buy beyond spending those same parameters on widening the diagonal inner branch?

Our first round of experiments gave a clean "yes" for wedge. At 10 epochs on ImageNet-1k, Full (Inner + Wedge) beat a parameter-matched Inner-wide control (zero bivectors) by +2.63%, and on CIFAR-100 it led by +1.05%. When we looked inside the activations and trained to 100 epochs, that interpretation fell apart. The original formulation starved the inner branch while leaving the wedge branch untouched, making a seemingly fair parameter-matched ablation misleading.

1. How a "fair" ablation starved the inner branch

Inside each subspace block, the original paper forms the context branch by applying an unnormalized 3×3 depthwise convolution to produce z2, then taking a discrete difference v = z2 − u. Because both branches receive the same pair (u, v), the comparison looks symmetric.

Inner branch:   pinner = SiLU( u ⊙ (z2 − u) ) = SiLU( u ⊙ z2 − u2 )
Wedge branch:   pwedge = u ∧ (z2 − u) = u ∧ z2   (since u ∧ u = 0)

Those two equations respond very differently when z2 is smaller than u. In exact arithmetic, subtracting u does nothing to the wedge product because u ∧ u = 0. In the inner branch, however, the −u2 term pulls the pre-activation strongly negative. Whenever u ⊙ v < −1.28, SiLU(x) flattens toward zero and its gradient shrinks. On trained checkpoints under the original diff design, 50% to 55% of deep-stage inner inputs sat below −1.28, inflating the energy ratio |Wedge| / |Dot| to 18.3×.

Adding normalization after the depthwise context layer (ctx_bn) and switching to ctx_mode="abs" (v = z2) leaves the mathematical wedge product unchanged while lifting the inner branch out of the negative tail. Try dragging the context scale below to see the asymmetry directly.

2. The matched 2×2 control: what wedge actually buys

Comparing our earlier 10-epoch v1 runs directly against 100-epoch v2 runs mixes two changes: the dead-zone fix and the longer schedule (plus macro changes like 7×7 convolutions). To isolate the interaction cleanly, we ran a strictly matched 2×2 control on CIFAR-100 across three identical random seeds (42, 101, 202; 12 runs total). All four cells share the exact same backbone, 5×5 spatial diffusion, projection bias, drop-path rate (0.20), and 200-epoch schedule, changing only context normalization/subtraction and whether the capacity goes into wedge products (Full) or a wider diagonal branch (Inner-wide).

Alongside that 2×2 table, we compare the 100-epoch ImageNet-1k Atto models (~3.6M parameters) after the dead-zone fix. Switch the metric tabs below to see how the conclusion changes between clean accuracy and structured perturbations.

On clean Top-1, fixing the dead-zone flips the CIFAR-100 result across all three seeds, from +0.88% ± 0.27% for Full to −0.68% ± 0.30% (paired interaction +1.56% ± 0.55%), and brings 100-epoch ImageNet-1k Full (71.08% 50k / 71.25% EMA) and Inner-wide (71.14% / 71.33% EMA) to a tie. On FFT phase-only and feature effective rank, Full keeps a repeatable lead across all three CIFAR-100 seeds and on 100-epoch ImageNet-1k.

Two readings follow from Figure 2. First, claiming that wedge products improve clean classification over an equal-parameter diagonal gating branch does not hold once both branches receive healthy inputs: on CIFAR-100, lifting Stage-3 dead-zone rates from 15% to 38% down to ~6% raises Inner-wide by +1.86% (77.65% to 79.51%), flipping a 3-for-3 win for Full into a 3-for-3 win for Inner-wide, and on 100-epoch ImageNet-1k the two tie (71.08% vs. 71.14%). Second, calling wedge products useless misses the structural signal: even after the dead-zone fix, antisymmetric bivectors consistently yield higher effective rank (+0.021 on CIFAR-100 across 3/3 seeds; 0.751 vs. 0.716 on ImageNet-1k) and higher FFT phase-only accuracy (+1.61% on CIFAR-100 across 3/3 seeds; +1.48% on ImageNet-1k, alongside +1.17% low-contrast and +1.29% Gaussian-noise margins on ImageNet-1k). Wedge changes what the network represents, even when clean Top-1 looks flat.

Notice also that ConvNeXt-Atto scores higher than both Subspace-CAN variants on FFT phase-only (46.52% vs. 35.78%), which points to its macro block design (100% per-token LayerNorm2d and an explicit FFN).

3. Scaling past 8M parameters: why BatchNorm + wedge explodes

Paper 2 replaced the per-token LayerNorm2d of Paper 1 with BatchNorm2d for speed. At Atto scale (~3.6M), training stayed stable. Once we scaled the same v2 architecture up to Pico (8.5M) and Nano (14.7M) in bf16, validation began collapsing to val/loss = NaN on 38% to 42% of epochs (34/90 on Pico, 38/90 on Nano), with peak wedge activations spiking to 3,466 and 5,156.

The root cause is a simple scaling mismatch. BatchNorm2d normalizes each channel by variance averaged over the entire batch and spatial grid (B × H × W ≈ 800,000 tokens). A single spatial outlier of magnitude M passes through BatchNorm2d almost untouched. Feeding it into a degree-2 bilinear product u ∧ v squares the spike to O(M2). That burst corrupts downstream BatchNorm2d running variance, and because our EMA step copies buffers directly into the EMA model, validation loss turns into NaN.

Two fixes stop the loop. Keeping BatchNorm2d and normalizing one side of the wedge to unit RMS per k-group (one-sided sphere norm, u ∧ (v / ||v||)) drops the output from degree 2 to degree 1 (O(M)), cutting peak wedge from 3,466 to 55.7 with 0/64 NaNs. Replacing BatchNorm2d with per-token RMSNorm2d bounds both sides (O(1)), clamping peak wedge to 64.4 with 0/90 NaNs even at 2× higher learning rate (4.0e-3).

One practical detail: every single run in this study used ortho_weight = 0.0. Earlier CliffordNet prototypes added an auxiliary orthogonality loss to prevent collinearity and catastrophic cancellation in bf16. With per-token normalization and fp32 FMA accumulation inside the wedge kernel, near-cancellation (relative difference < 10−2) naturally stays around 0.4%, so the auxiliary loss is unnecessary.

4. Fused Triton kernel: micro-benchmark vs. end-to-end reality

Because Paper 2 did not release a fused kernel, we wrote a recomputation-based Triton kernel for k ∈ {2, 3, 4} that saves only (Z1, Z2) for backward and computes wedge products with fp32 FMA. On the paper's benchmark tensor (64, 128, 64, 64) in bf16 NHWC, the isolated kernel runs 9.57× faster than torch.compile (and 58.6× faster than PyTorch eager).

Single-operator speedups rarely transfer one-to-one into full training speed. When we first plugged the Triton kernel into Subspace-CAN-v2-Pico, end-to-end training with torch.compile was only +4.3% faster than pure torch.compile (3,469 vs. 3,325 img/s), even though it already saved 19% peak VRAM. Profiling exposed the culprit: calling .chunk(2, dim=1) on a channels_last (NHWC) tensor produces non-contiguous strided views, forcing the Triton backward pass onto a slow strided fallback that spent 25.8% of total CUDA time copying memory.

Splitting the single 1×1 projection into two contiguous 1×1 convolutions (split_in_proj=True) eliminates the strided view with identical parameter count and math. End-to-end throughput then jumps to 4,434 img/s on Pico (+33.4% over torch.compile, 2.96× over eager) and 7,486 img/s on Atto (+33.9% over torch.compile) while cutting peak VRAM nearly in half vs. eager.

5. Practical takeaways

  • Matching parameter counts does not guarantee a fair ablation when a shared preprocessing step (here, v = z2 − u without context normalization) pushes one branch into a nonlinearity's dead-zone while leaving the other algebraically invariant. Inspect per-branch activation distributions before interpreting accuracy gaps.
  • Once the inner branch is healthy, pure diagonal gating matches or beats full Clifford blocks on clean accuracy (+1.56 ± 0.55% matched 2×2 interaction on CIFAR-100; tie at 71.1% on 100-epoch ImageNet-1k). Antisymmetric wedge features keep a repeatable edge on feature effective rank and FFT phase-only robustness across both datasets.
  • Mixing degree-2 bilinear products with BatchNorm2d breaks past 8M parameters because single-token outliers get squared into BN running statistics. Per-token RMSNorm2d (or one-sided sphere normalization when BN is required) eliminates the NaNs without auxiliary orthogonality losses.
  • An isolated 9.6× Triton kernel still loses most of its end-to-end gain if a preceding .chunk() breaks NHWC contiguity. Replacing it with contiguous projections (split_in_proj) turns that memory reduction into a +33% full-step throughput gain over torch.compile.
Experimental protocol, hardware, and reproducibility notes

All experiments were executed in PyTorch 2.10 + Triton 3.6 on an 8× NVIDIA H800 node across 70 total runs (42 CIFAR-100 200-epoch single-GPU runs in fp32; 28 ImageNet-1k 10, 25, 90, and 100-epoch DDP runs in bf16-mixed with Global BS = 1024, AdamW, cosine decay, Mixup 0.8, CutMix 1.0, EMA 0.9998, and ortho_weight = 0.0). Exact parameter parity with Tables 2 to 4 of arXiv:2610.37226 is verified in tests/test_subspace_models.py, and Triton forward/backward numerical parity (including a CUDA gridY > 65,535 fix needed at Nano scale) is verified in tests/test_triton_subspace.py.
